We threaten something big and far away, then we're too tired to follow through. Kids learn the threat is noise, and the shouting gets louder. A prod is different.

It keeps you calm

You can't prod someone with a red foam ball while shouting without looking ridiculous. The ritual stops the escalation before it starts.

There's nothing to argue with

The prod says it for you. No speech to push back against, just a sad sound and a number going down.

It happens face to face

Not a tap on an app from the sofa. A prod is contact and eye contact, in the room, in the moment.

Praise becomes a ceremony

“Well done” feels thin. A knighting with a fanfare is a moment a kid will talk about, and want again.

Meet them where they play

Kids spend hours in worlds where every action gets instant feedback. Take a hit, lose health. Do something brave, level up. Real life gives them the opposite: vague consequences, days later. Prodly borrows the rules of their games and brings them into the room, triggered by something physical and human.

A prod is a hit.

Their health bar drops, right now, with a sound they recognise. No lecture needed; they already understand damage.

A knighting is a level-up.

Every knighting moves them up the ranks, from Squire to Knight to Paladin. Status is what they play for anyway.

Sunday is a new season.

The week resets, like every game they love. A bad week isn't forever, and a good one is worth defending.

      Why it works

      It isn't a gimmick. It's three well-studied ideas in one household tool.

      Empty threats teach kids to ignore you

      A 2024 national poll of 725 US parents of young children found threats are a go-to tactic, with a quarter threatening no Santa or gifts. The poll's co-director warned that empty threats “undermine trust and credibility.” An earlier home-observation study linked mothers not following through to more conduct problems in preschoolers.

      University of Michigan Health C.S. Mott Children's Hospital National Poll on Children's Health, 2024. Journal of Abnormal Child Psychology, 1989.

      Losing hurts more than winning pleases

      Loss aversion is one of the best-established findings in behavioural economics. In a Chicago Heights field trial, teachers paid a bonus up front, and asked to hand it back if results didn't improve, raised maths scores by 0.2 to 0.4 standard deviations. The same bonus paid afterwards made no significant difference.

      Kahneman & Tversky, 1979. Fryer, Levitt, List & Sadoff, NBER Working Paper 18237, 2012.

      Taking tokens away works with kids

      Psychologists call it “response cost”: children hold tokens and lose them for misbehaviour. Classroom studies find it cuts disruptive behaviour about as well as rewards do, and teachers tend to prefer it. Using both together, as Prodly does, is a standard approach.

      McGoey & DuPaul, School Psychology Quarterly, 2000. Studies in the Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis.

      In fairness: when the teacher trial was repeated and the two years pooled, the effect was smaller overall, strong in the first year and absent in the second (Fryer et al., American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 2022).
